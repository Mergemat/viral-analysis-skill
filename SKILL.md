# Viral Analysis

Viral analysis uses story friction: the pressure that makes an audience continue because a specific person, action, choice, or refusal sits under visible tension, with a consequence or unanswered question.

Treat content as a first promise, not as information. The audience does not owe attention to an idea; they follow a situation. This applies to any content surface where someone decides whether to continue: video, post, essay, ad, pitch, email, landing page, script, lesson, or product narrative.

## Steps

1. Build the evidence stack before judging.
   Collect the target content, its primary surface, available performance/context, creator or account context, 2-5 working comparables, and 2-5 category or culture comparables. If a linked video needs direct inspection, download it with `yt-dlp` and analyze the file, not only the page metadata or thumbnail. Prefer the creator's own winners over generic best practice. If working examples are missing, say that the comparison is category-based. Completion: every judgment has at least one of these anchors: target evidence, creator winner, category pattern, or culture pattern.

2. Name the package before judging quality.
   Identify the asset, visible package, intended audience, cold-viewer or cold-reader first promise, and comparison set. Completion: one sentence says, "This is being packaged as X, while its stronger opportunity is Y."

3. Diagnose the first promise.
   Read the first screen, line, beat, frame, or section as if no outside context will be read. Strong promise: concrete person, behavior, choice, refusal, relationship, contradiction, consequence. Weak promise: topic, mood, nostalgia, worldview, abstract debate, polished beauty, explanation. Completion: one sentence says, "The opening says X, not Y."

4. Extract the working pattern.
   Compare the target against the working comparables on first promise, visible conflict, specificity, relationship dynamic, open loop, payoff, and cultural fit. Do not copy the winner's surface; name the mechanism it proves. Completion: write "The winners do A -> B -> C; this piece does X -> Y -> Z."

5. Separate asset from packaging.
   Do not blame length, medium, production quality, subtitles, platform, or topic until the first promise has been tested. The asset can be strong while the package sells the wrong thing. Completion: name the bottleneck: asset, package, audience fit, cultural timing, or execution.

6. Convert belief into a moment.
   Replace "this idea is true" with "someone did, chose, refused, lost, hid, discovered, or misunderstood X, causing Y." Move broad theses after the audience already cares. Completion: write a one-shot frame with a specific subject, concrete action, and implied consequence.

7. Build the friction ladder.
   Order the content as: specific behavior -> tension or contrast -> emotional explanation -> moral, practical, comic, strategic, or narrative payoff. Each segment must sharpen the situation, raise the consequence, or pay off the tension. Completion: there is a reason to continue at the opening, middle, and end.

8. Read culture as format pressure.
   Culture is not only values or aesthetics; it is the current set of rewarded promises. Identify what the audience is rewarding now, what feels flat, and what transferable pattern can be borrowed without copying surface style. Completion: one sentence says, "Current culture rewards X here; Y is flatter; borrow Z."

9. Prescribe repackaging, not generic improvement.
   Keep the useful asset where possible. Change the first promise, sequence, framing, or contrast before proposing a full remake. Give specific replacement lines, scenes, sections, or beats. Completion: recommendations can be executed without another interpretation pass.

## Working Comparison

Treat working examples as controls, not inspiration. The job is to identify why the target loses to them under the same audience conditions.

For each working comparable, extract:

- First promise: what the audience understands before context.
- Situation: who is doing what.
- Friction: choice, refusal, contradiction, risk, or consequence.
- Payoff: what the audience waits to learn or feel.
- Surface: what is format-specific and should not be copied blindly.

Then write the delta:

- "Winner opens with concrete behavior; target opens with topic."
- "Winner has relationship dynamics on the surface; target hides the argument in explanation."
- "Winner creates a question; target asks for agreement."
- "Winner turns belief into a scene; target keeps belief as commentary."

## Media Handling

When the source is a video link and the visual sequence matters, use `yt-dlp` to download the video if needed. Judge first promise, cuts, pacing, subtitles, and payoff from the actual media file whenever possible. If download fails or access is blocked, say so and base the diagnosis on the available surface evidence.

## Judgment Principles

- Story first, content second: the song, essay, pitch, opinion, product, or idea is the asset; the situation is the hook.
- Conflict before agreement: do not ask the audience to agree with a worldview before giving them a scene to follow.
- Specific beats broad: "a 7-year-old chooses an iPad over the playground" beats "gadgets changed childhood."
- Uncomfortable before beautiful: start with the moment that creates need, not the most polished image or sentence.
- Primary surface carries the hook: if the core promise hides in a caption, footnote, aside, or context, the work is making people do extra work.
- Length is neutral until friction fails: long can work with open loops; short can fail if passive.
- Culture is directional, not copied: extract the winning mechanism, not the costume.

## Ideal Content

Ideal content opens as a situation already in motion. Someone wants, chooses, refuses, loses, hides, discovers, or misunderstands something. The audience sees enough to form a question and not enough to close it. The body dramatizes the stakes. The ending gives a payoff: moral, emotional, practical, comic, strategic, or narrative.

## Diagnostic Voice

Use crisp contrast:

- "Not weak because X. Weak because Y."
- "The issue is not X; the first promise is Y."
- "This asks people to agree. Stronger content makes them wait for the answer."
- "The asset is A, but the package sells B."
- "Do not copy the surface. Borrow the mechanism."

## Anti-Patterns

- Mood piece trying to carry a story job.
- Essay topic presented where a scene is needed.
- Beautiful opening with no conflict.
- Question that feels like a discussion prompt instead of a story start.
- Caption, subtitle, or explanation doing the hook's job.
- Audience must wait for explanation before caring.
- Content polished enough to lose rawness but not dramatic enough to become an event.
- Culture read as "what aesthetic is trending" instead of "what promise is rewarded."
